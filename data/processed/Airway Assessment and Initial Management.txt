Airway Assessment and Initial Management

1. Purpose

This document provides a structured framework for prehospital assessment and initial management of patients with actual or potential airway compromise.

This document is intended as an educational and clinical-reference resource. Local EMS protocols, medical-director orders, scope of practice, manufacturer instructions, and applicable law take precedence over this document.

Airway management should be approached as an organized system of care that includes assessment, oxygenation, ventilation, monitoring, airway-device selection, confirmation of airway placement, rescue strategies, and ongoing reassessment. NAEMSP recommends addressing hypoxemia and hypotension before advanced airway attempts when possible and using continuous physiologic monitoring during airway management.

2. Airway Assessment

Primary questions

Assess:

Is the airway currently patent?

Can the patient maintain airway patency independently?

Can the patient adequately oxygenate?

Can the patient adequately ventilate?

Are protective airway reflexes intact?

Is there evidence of impending airway deterioration?

Is a basic airway intervention sufficient?

Is an advanced airway intervention indicated under the applicable protocol?

Airway assessment should occur immediately in patients with respiratory distress, altered mental status, trauma, cardiac arrest, or other conditions that may compromise airway patency or ventilation.

3. Signs of Airway Compromise

Potential findings include:

Inability to speak normally

Abnormal or absent air movement

Gurgling

Snoring respirations

Stridor

Severe upper-airway obstruction

Blood, vomitus, secretions, or foreign material obstructing the airway

Facial or airway trauma

Swelling of the tongue, pharynx, or airway

Burns or suspected inhalation injury

Decreased level of consciousness with loss of airway tone

Cyanosis or other evidence of severe hypoxemia

Increasing fatigue or inability to maintain adequate ventilation

A patient may initially maintain airway patency but subsequently deteriorate. Reassessment is therefore required throughout patient care.

4. Airway vs. Breathing Problem

Airway and breathing problems frequently occur together but should be assessed separately.

Airway problem

The primary problem is inability to maintain or protect a patent airway.

Examples include:

Tongue obstruction

Secretions or blood

Vomitus

Foreign-body obstruction

Upper-airway edema

Facial or airway trauma

Decreased consciousness

Breathing problem

The airway may be patent, but ventilation or oxygenation is inadequate.

Examples include:

Severe asthma

COPD exacerbation

Pulmonary edema

Pneumonia

Opioid-associated respiratory depression

Neuromuscular weakness

Severe metabolic illness

A patient may have both an airway and breathing problem simultaneously.

5. Immediate Initial Interventions

When airway compromise is identified, treatment should begin while assessment continues.

Potential interventions include:

Positioning the patient to optimize airway patency

Manual airway maneuvers

Removal of visible airway obstruction when appropriate

Suctioning blood, vomitus, or secretions

Oropharyngeal airway when appropriate

Nasopharyngeal airway when appropriate

Supplemental oxygen when indicated

Assisted ventilation with a bag-valve-mask when spontaneous ventilation is inadequate

Noninvasive ventilation when appropriate and permitted by protocol

Supraglottic airway or endotracheal airway when indicated and within provider scope

Rescue airway techniques when the initial airway strategy fails

The least invasive intervention that adequately maintains oxygenation and ventilation may be appropriate; the choice of intervention should depend on patient condition, clinician capability, available equipment, and applicable protocol.

6. Positioning

Positioning should be used to optimize airway patency, oxygenation, and ventilation while accounting for the patient's clinical condition and mechanism of injury.

Consider:

Head and neck position

Body habitus

Pregnancy

Trauma considerations

Cervical spine considerations

Obesity

Airway anatomy

Respiratory distress

Need for airway intervention

For advanced airway management, optimizing physiology before airway insertion is important. NAEMSP recommends correcting or preventing hypoxemia and hypotension before advanced airway attempts when possible.

7. Suction

Suction should be immediately available when airway contamination is possible.

Potential indications include:

Blood

Vomitus

Excessive secretions

Foreign material

Other material interfering with ventilation or airway visualization

Suction should be used when needed to maintain airway patency and facilitate effective ventilation or airway management.

Repeated suctioning should not unnecessarily delay effective oxygenation and ventilation.

8. Basic Airway Adjuncts

Oropharyngeal Airway

An OPA may be considered when loss of airway tone causes upper-airway obstruction and the patient does not have an intact gag reflex.

Consider:

Level of consciousness

Airway reflexes

Oral trauma

Correct sizing

Correct placement

Ongoing reassessment

Nasopharyngeal Airway

An NPA may be considered for selected patients who require an airway adjunct but cannot tolerate an OPA.

Consider:

Facial trauma

Suspected basilar skull injury

Nasal pathology or obstruction

Bleeding risk

Local protocol and contraindications

Exact indications, contraindications, sizing, and insertion technique should follow the applicable EMS protocol and device instructions.

9. Oxygenation and Ventilation

Oxygenation and ventilation are different physiologic goals.

Oxygenation

Oxygenation concerns the amount of oxygen available to the patient.

Assessment may include:

Pulse oximetry

Clinical appearance

Skin findings

Work of breathing

Mental status

Ventilation

Ventilation concerns removal of carbon dioxide and movement of air.

Assessment may include:

Respiratory rate

Respiratory effort

Tidal volume

Chest movement

Mental status

End-tidal carbon dioxide when available

A patient can have inadequate ventilation even when pulse oximetry appears acceptable, particularly when supplemental oxygen is being administered.

10. Bag-Valve-Mask Ventilation

BVM ventilation should be considered when a patient is apneic or has inadequate spontaneous ventilation.

Effective BVM ventilation requires:

A patent airway

An adequate mask seal

Appropriate positioning

Appropriate ventilation technique

Adequate oxygen source when available

Continuous reassessment of effectiveness

Signs of effective ventilation may include:

Visible chest rise

Improving clinical appearance

Appropriate oxygenation

Appropriate exhaled carbon dioxide when monitored

Two-person BVM techniques may improve mask seal and ventilation effectiveness in difficult circumstances.

Avoid unnecessary excessive ventilation. Ventilation should be appropriate for the patient's clinical condition and applicable protocol.

11. Advanced Airway Consideration

An advanced airway may be considered when basic airway interventions cannot adequately maintain:

Airway patency

Oxygenation

Ventilation

Protection from aspiration

Advanced airway options may include:

Supraglottic airway

Endotracheal tube

Drug-assisted airway management

Surgical airway in appropriately indicated circumstances

The decision should consider the patient's condition, anticipated clinical course, provider competency, available equipment, transport environment, and local protocol.

12. Confirmation of an Advanced Airway

Advanced airway placement must be confirmed using objective methods.

Waveform capnography is strongly supported for initial and ongoing confirmation of advanced airway placement. NAEMSP specifically recommends initial and continuous waveform-capnography confirmation for advanced airways and emphasizes continuous monitoring after placement.

Assessment may also include:

Waveform capnography

Chest rise

Bilateral breath sounds

Absence of major air leak

Clinical response

Other objective confirmation methods specified by local protocol

A single sign should not automatically be treated as definitive confirmation when more reliable objective methods are available.

13. Supraglottic Airways

Supraglottic airways can function as primary or rescue airway devices depending on the EMS system and clinical circumstances.

NAEMSP recommends that EMS systems using endotracheal intubation also have appropriately selected SGA devices and ensure clinician training and competency. Initial and ongoing SGA placement confirmation with waveform capnography is strongly encouraged.

If an SGA is functioning effectively, conversion to an endotracheal tube should not automatically occur. The decision should account for the patient's condition, effectiveness of ventilation, and clinical circumstances.

14. Endotracheal Intubation

Endotracheal intubation is an advanced airway intervention requiring appropriate training, credentialing, equipment, and ongoing competency assessment.

Before attempting intubation, consider:

Patient physiology

Oxygenation

Ventilation

Hemodynamics

Positioning

Preoxygenation

Equipment readiness

Backup airway strategy

Number of anticipated attempts

Rescue plan

Airway attempts should not be allowed to cause prolonged hypoxemia or other avoidable physiologic deterioration.

15. Failed Airway

Every advanced airway attempt should have a predefined rescue strategy.

If an advanced airway attempt fails, priorities include:

Recognize failure promptly.

Re-establish effective oxygenation and ventilation.

Reassess the airway.

Correct reversible problems.

Use an alternative airway strategy when indicated.

Escalate to a rescue airway when appropriate.

Continue objective physiologic monitoring.

Potential rescue options include:

Improved BVM ventilation

OPA/NPA

Supraglottic airway

Alternative intubation approach when appropriate

Surgical airway when indicated and authorized

A failed intubation does not mean that ventilation has failed. The immediate priority remains adequate oxygenation and ventilation.

16. Surgical Airway

A surgical airway is a rescue intervention for selected situations in which the airway cannot be adequately secured or ventilated by less invasive means.

NAEMSP describes surgical airway as an important component of advanced airway algorithms and recommends that appropriately credentialed EMS systems have the capability to perform it when indicated.

Indications, contraindications, equipment, technique, and credentialing requirements must come from the applicable local protocol and training program.

17. Drug-Assisted Airway Management

Drug-assisted airway management may be used by appropriately trained and credentialed EMS clinicians when authorized by the applicable system.

Drug-assisted airway management requires:

Appropriate patient selection

Airway assessment

Adequate preoxygenation

Physiologic monitoring

Appropriate medication selection

Backup airway equipment

Rescue-airway capability

Post-airway analgesia/sedation when applicable

Waveform-capnography confirmation

Ongoing monitoring

NAEMSP emphasizes that systems performing drug-assisted airway management should have clear guidelines, adequate training, physician oversight, and the ability to manage failed airway attempts.

Medication names, concentrations, doses, contraindications, and administration procedures should be taken from the current local EMS medication/protocol documents rather than this general reference.

18. Special Populations

Airway management may require modified assessment and intervention in:

Pediatric patients

Neonates

Geriatric patients

Patients with obesity

Pregnant patients

Trauma patients

Patients with facial or neck trauma

Patients with burns or suspected inhalation injury

Patients with severe anaphylaxis

Patients with foreign-body airway obstruction

These populations should have dedicated protocol documents in the Airway folder.

19. Ongoing Reassessment

Airway management does not end when an airway device is inserted.

Continuously reassess:

Airway patency

Respiratory effort

Chest movement

Oxygen saturation

End-tidal CO₂/waveform when available

Breath sounds

Hemodynamics

Mental status

Airway-device position

Ventilation effectiveness

Patient response

Advanced airway devices can become displaced after initially successful placement. Continuous monitoring is therefore important.

20. Documentation Requirements

Airway documentation should include, when applicable:

Initial airway assessment

Initial respiratory status

Oxygenation and ventilation findings

Airway interventions performed

Airway adjunct/device used

Device size

Number of attempts

Time of airway placement

Confirmation method

Waveform capnography findings

Oxygen saturation

Ventilation method

Complications

Rescue interventions

Patient response

Ongoing airway reassessment

Provider identification/credentialing where required

21. AI Retrieval Rules

When this document is used by an AI system:

Priority 1 — Local protocol

If a current agency, county, state, or medical-director protocol is available, use it as the controlling source for:

Indications

Contraindications

Medication dosing

Device selection

Scope of practice

Number of attempts

Specific procedures

Transport requirements

Medical-control requirements

Priority 2 — Current clinical guidance

Use recognized EMS/medical organizations and current clinical literature for general evidence and educational context.

NAEMSP maintains a dedicated collection of prehospital airway resources covering airway education, drug-assisted airway management, manual and mechanical ventilation, pediatric airway management, supraglottic airways, surgical airways, and airway physiology.

Priority 3 — General medical knowledge

General model knowledge should not override a specific current local protocol.

When documentation is insufficient

If the supplied documentation does not contain an answer, the AI should explicitly state:

"The provided documentation does not contain enough information to answer this reliably."

The AI should not invent a protocol, medication dose, contraindication, or scope-of-practice rule.

22. Safety Rule

This reference is not a substitute for current EMS protocols, medical direction, formal airway training, competency verification, or clinical judgment.

When a conflict exists between this document and a current authoritative local protocol, the current authoritative protocol takes precedence.
