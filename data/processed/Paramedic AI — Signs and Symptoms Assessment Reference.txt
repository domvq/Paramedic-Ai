Paramedic AI — Signs and Symptoms Assessment Reference

1. Purpose

Signs and symptoms are the foundation of prehospital patient assessment.

A symptom is something reported or experienced by the patient.

Examples:

Pain

Nausea

Dizziness

Shortness of breath

Weakness

Palpitations

A sign is an observable or measurable finding.

Examples:

Heart rate

Blood pressure

Respiratory rate

Oxygen saturation

Fever

Cyanosis

Altered mental status

Abnormal lung sounds

Skin color or temperature

The Paramedic AI should use signs and symptoms to identify possible clinical patterns while avoiding premature diagnostic conclusions.

2. General Assessment Principle

The AI should not ask:

"What disease causes this symptom?"

as its first step.

Instead:

Identify the chief complaint.

Determine whether the patient is immediately unstable.

Identify life threats.

Obtain relevant vital signs.

Assess airway, breathing, and circulation.

Determine onset and progression.

Identify associated signs and symptoms.

Consider relevant medical history.

Consider medications and allergies.

Develop a differential diagnosis.

Identify findings that support or contradict each possibility.

Determine whether immediate intervention or transport is required according to local protocol.

3. Primary Survey

The primary survey should identify immediate threats to life.

A — Airway

Assess:

Is the airway open?

Can the patient speak?

Is there obstruction?

Is there stridor?

Is there gurgling?

Is there snoring respirations?

Is there blood, vomit, or secretions?

Is there facial or airway trauma?

Is there swelling of the tongue or airway?

Is there evidence of anaphylaxis?

Concerning findings

Inability to speak

Severe airway obstruction

Stridor

Absent or severely diminished air movement

Altered consciousness with loss of airway protection

Severe facial/neck trauma

Progressive airway swelling

4. B — Breathing

Assess:

Respiratory rate

Respiratory effort

Respiratory depth

Chest expansion

SpO2

Skin color

Lung sounds

Ability to speak

Work of breathing

Accessory muscle use

Position of comfort

Concerning findings

Severe respiratory distress

Cyanosis

Apnea

Agonal respirations

Very slow or very rapid respirations

Poor chest movement

Severe accessory muscle use

Inability to speak normally

Altered mental status associated with respiratory distress

Significantly abnormal oxygenation

Remember:

Oxygenation and ventilation are different.

A patient can have an acceptable SpO2 while experiencing significant ventilatory failure.

Consider EtCO2/capnography when clinically appropriate and available.

8. Pain Assessment

Pain should be evaluated systematically.

Assess:

Location

Onset

Provocation/Palliation

Quality

Region/Radiation

Severity

Timing

A common framework is:

OPQRST

O — Onset

When did it begin?

Was onset:

Sudden?

Gradual?

During exertion?

At rest?

P — Provocation/Palliation

What makes it worse?

What makes it better?

Q — Quality

Examples:

Pressure

Tightness

Burning

Sharp

Stabbing

Tearing

Cramping

Aching

R — Region/Radiation

Where is the pain?

Does it radiate to:

Arm

Shoulder

Jaw

Back

Abdomen

Other locations?

S — Severity

Use an appropriate pain scale.

T — Time

When did it begin?

Has it changed?

Is it:

Constant?

Intermittent?

Getting worse?

Getting better?

9. Chest Pain

Chest discomfort may be caused by cardiac, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, vascular, or other conditions.

Important associated findings include:

Dyspnea

Diaphoresis

Nausea

Vomiting

Weakness

Syncope

Palpitations

Radiation to arm/jaw/back

Pleuritic pain

Positional pain

Exertional symptoms

Potential serious causes include:

Acute coronary syndrome

Aortic dissection

Pulmonary embolism

Pneumothorax

Pericardial disease

Esophageal rupture

Do not assume chest pain is cardiac or noncardiac based on a single symptom.

11. Wheezing

Wheezing generally indicates narrowed lower airways but has multiple possible causes.

Consider:

Asthma

COPD

Allergic reaction

Anaphylaxis

Pulmonary edema

Foreign-body aspiration

Other airway disease

Important:

Not every patient with wheezing has asthma.

Also recognize that a severely ill patient may have very little audible wheezing because airflow is critically reduced.

12. Crackles

Crackles may occur when air moves through fluid or abnormal small airways.

16. Stroke Signs and Symptoms

Common warning signs include:

Arm weakness

Speech difficulty

Sudden sensory loss

Sudden vision changes

Sudden severe dizziness or ataxia

Use the stroke assessment tool specified by the local EMS system.

Document:

Baseline neurologic status

A negative screening tool does not completely exclude stroke.

18. Syncope

Syncope is transient loss of consciousness caused by transient cerebral hypoperfusion with spontaneous recovery.

Important questions:

What was the patient doing immediately beforehand?

Was there exertion?

Was there chest pain?

Was there palpitations?

Was there dyspnea?

Was there a sudden severe headache?

Was there trauma?

Was there seizure-like activity?

Was there a prolonged postictal period?

Is there a cardiac history?

Is there a family history of sudden death?

Potential serious causes include:

Dysrhythmia

Acute coronary syndrome

Pulmonary embolism

Structural cardiac disease

Hemorrhage

Neurologic disease

44. General Red-Flag Pattern

The AI should increase concern when multiple systems show abnormalities simultaneously.

Examples:

Chest pain + diaphoresis + dyspnea

may raise concern for acute coronary syndrome.

Fever + altered mental status + hypotension

may raise concern for severe systemic infection/shock.

Wheezing + hypotension + airway swelling

may raise concern for anaphylaxis.

Altered mental status + abnormal respiratory pattern + pinpoint pupils

may raise concern for opioid toxicity.

These are patterns for differential diagnosis, not definitive diagnoses.

46. Vital Signs

The AI should evaluate trends rather than isolated values.

Assess:

Respiratory rate

50. AI Safety Rules

The AI should:

Distinguish signs from symptoms.

Identify immediate life threats before discussing differential diagnoses.

Avoid premature diagnostic closure.

Avoid treating one abnormal vital sign as a diagnosis.

Consider multiple organ systems.

Recognize that serious illness can present atypically.

Ask for missing information when it could change the assessment.

Consider age and relevant patient characteristics.

Consider medications and medical history.

Reassess after interventions.

Identify deterioration promptly.

Follow the applicable local EMS protocol.

Clearly distinguish clinical findings from diagnostic conclusions.

Cite the underlying source when using protocol-specific information.

Never invent vital signs, symptoms, physical findings, or medical history.

51. Core Principle

The goal of prehospital assessment is not simply to identify a disease.

The goal is to:

Recognize life threats → stabilize the patient → identify the most likely causes → identify dangerous alternatives → provide appropriate protocol-directed care → reassess → transport to the appropriate destination.

Signs and symptoms should therefore be interpreted as patterns, not isolated facts.
