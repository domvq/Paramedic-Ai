Paramedic AI — Prehospital Medications and Oxygen Reference

1. Purpose

This document provides a structured educational reference for commonly encountered prehospital medications and oxygen therapy.

It is intended to support:

Paramedic education

Medication recognition

Dose/reference lookup

Clinical reasoning

AI retrieval

Protocol comparison

Documentation support

This document is not a substitute for the current EMS agency protocol, medical director orders, drug formulary, or online medical direction.

Medication concentrations, permitted indications, routes, repeat intervals, maximum doses, and scope of practice vary between EMS systems.

The Paramedic AI should therefore treat the user's current local protocol as the highest-priority source.

2. Medication Safety Rules for the AI

Before providing a medication recommendation, the AI should determine:

Patient age

Adult versus pediatric patient

Weight when weight-based dosing is relevant

Indication

Allergies

Contraindications

Current vital signs

Relevant medical history

Current medications

Route available

Medication concentration

Local EMS protocol

Maximum permitted dose

Whether repeat dosing is permitted

Whether medical control is required

The AI should never calculate a dose from an unknown medication concentration.

For example:

Dose: 5 mg

does not automatically tell the paramedic how many mL to administer.

The AI must know the concentration:

5 mg/mL

before calculating:

5 mg ÷ 5 mg/mL = 1 mL

4. OXYGEN

Purpose

Oxygen is a medication and should be administered based on the patient's clinical condition and oxygenation/ventilation status.

Routine oxygen administration to every patient is not appropriate.

Oxygen should generally be considered when the patient has:

Hypoxemia

Respiratory distress

Respiratory failure

Significant clinical signs of inadequate oxygenation

Certain toxicologic emergencies

Cardiac arrest

Post-cardiac arrest care

Other protocol-defined indications

Current EMS reference material identifies supplemental oxygen as appropriate for respiratory distress, clinical markers of respiratory compromise, or inadequate measured oxygenation, while emphasizing assessment of oxygenation and ventilation.

5. Oxygen Saturation

Pulse oximetry should be interpreted with the clinical presentation.

The AI should never treat SpO2 as the only measure of respiratory status.

Important limitations include:

Poor peripheral perfusion

Motion artifact

Nail products

Equipment problems

Dyshemoglobinemias

Carbon monoxide exposure

Pulse oximetry can be misleading in carbon monoxide poisoning because the measured saturation may not accurately represent tissue oxygenation.

6. Nasal Cannula

Typical flow:

1–6 L/min

Approximate FiO2:

24–44%

Actual FiO2 varies with:

Patient respiratory rate

Tidal volume

Mouth breathing

Nasal obstruction

Device fit

Common use:

Mild hypoxemia

Patients who need supplemental oxygen but do not require a high-concentration device

Patients who can maintain their airway and ventilate adequately

7. Simple Face Mask

Typical flow:

5–12 L/min

Approximate FiO2:

35–55%

Never use a simple face mask at a flow that is too low to adequately clear exhaled carbon dioxide from the mask.

8. Partial-Rebreather Mask

Typical flow:

8–15 L/min

Approximate FiO2:

35–60%

Actual delivered oxygen concentration varies with:

Flow

Mask seal

Patient minute ventilation

9. Non-Rebreather Mask

Typical flow:

8–15 L/min

Approximate FiO2:

60–95%

The reservoir should remain appropriately inflated during inspiration.

Actual FiO2 varies according to:

Mask seal

Flow rate

Patient respiratory pattern

10. Venturi Mask

Typical flow:

Approximately 4–15 L/min depending on the adapter

Approximate FiO2:

24–50%

Venturi systems are designed to deliver a more controlled oxygen concentration than simple variable-performance masks.

Use the manufacturer's specified flow rate for the selected FiO2 adapter.

11. Bag-Valve Mask

A BVM is primarily a ventilation device rather than simply an oxygen-delivery device.

When supplemental oxygen is available:

Oxygen flow: commonly 15 L/min

A reservoir-equipped BVM can provide a high inspired oxygen concentration when properly assembled and ventilated.

The AI should distinguish:

Oxygenation problem

from

Ventilation problem.

A patient with inadequate ventilation may require assisted ventilation rather than simply increasing oxygen flow.

12. Oxygen in Cardiac Arrest

During active cardiac arrest, oxygenation and ventilation are priorities.

Follow the current resuscitation algorithm and local EMS protocol.

The 2025 AHA guidelines provide current recommendations for adult and pediatric resuscitation, including oxygenation and ventilation.

13. Oxygen After ROSC

After return of spontaneous circulation:

Avoid hypoxemia.

Initially use high-concentration oxygen when reliable oxygenation cannot yet be measured.

Once reliable oxygen saturation is available, titrate oxygen according to current post-cardiac-arrest recommendations.

The 2025 AHA guideline recommends 100% inspired oxygen until SpO2 or PaO2 can be reliably measured, followed by titration to avoid both hypoxemia and hyperoxemia.

14. ASPIRIN

Class

Antiplatelet agent.

Common prehospital indication

Suspected acute coronary syndrome when appropriate.

Typical adult dose

162–325 mg PO

Usually administered as chewable aspirin when appropriate.

Important considerations

Assess for:

Aspirin allergy

Active significant bleeding

Major contraindications

Inability to safely swallow

Local ACS protocol

The exact dose and contraindications should follow the applicable EMS protocol.

15. NITROGLYCERIN

Class

Nitrate / vasodilator.

Common indication

Suspected ischemic chest discomfort when appropriate.

Important considerations

Before administration evaluate:

Blood pressure

Suspected right ventricular infarction

Recent phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor use

Significant volume depletion

Other protocol-specific contraindications

The AI should never recommend nitroglycerin solely because the patient reports "chest pain."

16. EPINEPHRINE — ANAPHYLAXIS

Class

Sympathomimetic / adrenergic agonist.

Common indication

Anaphylaxis with significant systemic involvement.

Adult IM reference dose

0.3–0.5 mg IM

Using:

1 mg/mL (1:1,000)

The exact dose and repeat interval should follow the applicable EMS protocol.

Pediatric

Weight-based dosing is generally used.

A commonly referenced dose is:

0.01 mg/kg IM

using the appropriate concentration.

Maximum dose and repeat interval depend on protocol and patient age/size.

Important principle

For suspected anaphylaxis, epinephrine should not be unnecessarily delayed while waiting for secondary medications.

17. EPINEPHRINE — CARDIAC ARREST

Cardiac-arrest dosing is different from anaphylaxis dosing.

The AI must never confuse:

Epinephrine 1 mg/mL

with

Epinephrine 0.1 mg/mL

The concentration must always be explicitly displayed before administration.

Current AHA adult ALS guidelines include epinephrine as a vasopressor therapy during cardiac arrest.

Use the current AHA/local cardiac-arrest algorithm for:

Dose

Route

Repeat interval

Concentration

18. ALBUTEROL

Class

Beta-2 adrenergic bronchodilator.

Common indications

Bronchospasm

Asthma exacerbation

COPD exacerbation

Wheezing associated with bronchospasm

Typical nebulized adult dose

Common EMS formulations include:

2.5 mg nebulized

or

5 mg nebulized

depending on the protocol and formulation.

Some systems use continuous or repeated nebulization for severe bronchospasm.

Important considerations

Monitor for:

Tachycardia

Tremor

Anxiety

Hypokalemia

Palpitations

Dose and formulation must be verified against the medication actually stocked by the EMS system.

19. IPRATROPIUM

Class

Anticholinergic bronchodilator.

Common indication

Moderate/severe bronchospasm, often combined with albuterol.

A common nebulized adult dose is:

0.5 mg nebulized

The exact indication, dose, and repeat policy depend on local protocol.

20. NALOXONE

Class

Opioid antagonist.

Common indication

Suspected opioid-induced respiratory depression.

The goal is restoration of adequate ventilation and airway protection rather than necessarily producing complete awakening.

Common routes include:

Intranasal

Intramuscular

Intravenous

Intraosseous

Dose depends on:

Product concentration

Route

Patient response

Local protocol

The AI should prioritize adequate ventilation and airway management rather than presenting naloxone as a replacement for ventilation.

The 2025 AHA BLS guidelines include opioid antagonists in the management of suspected opioid overdose and respiratory/cardiac arrest.

21. DEXTROSE

Class

Carbohydrate / glucose replacement.

Common indication

Clinically significant hypoglycemia with altered mental status or inability to safely take oral glucose.

Common concentrations

EMS systems may stock different concentrations, including:

D10

D25

D50

These are not interchangeable by volume.

For example:

D10 = 0.1 g/mL

D25 = 0.25 g/mL

D50 = 0.5 g/mL

The AI must calculate the required volume from the concentration actually available.

Example

If the desired dose is 10 g and the available solution is D10:

10 g ÷ 0.1 g/mL =

100 mL

Always verify the local protocol before administration.

22. GLUCAGON

Class

Hormone that increases blood glucose through hepatic glycogenolysis.

Common indication

Hypoglycemia when IV/IO access is unavailable or when oral glucose cannot safely be administered, depending on protocol.

Dose depends on:

Adult versus pediatric patient

Product formulation

Local protocol

Follow the medication package instructions and local EMS protocol.

23. ONDANSETRON

Class

5-HT3 receptor antagonist.

Common indication

Nausea and vomiting.

Common adult EMS dosing may include:

4 mg

Routes may include:

PO/ODT

IV

IM

depending on formulation and protocol.

Important considerations include:

QT prolongation

Drug interactions

Existing arrhythmia risk

24. DIPHENHYDRAMINE

Class

First-generation antihistamine.

Common indications

May be used for selected allergic reactions or other protocol-defined indications.

Important adverse effects include:

Sedation

Anticholinergic effects

Tachycardia

Confusion

Urinary retention

In anaphylaxis, antihistamines are adjunctive therapy and should not replace epinephrine.

25. FENTANYL

Class

Opioid analgesic.

Common indication

Moderate-to-severe pain when appropriate.

Dose is generally protocol-specific and should be determined by:

Patient weight

Pain severity

Age

Respiratory status

Hemodynamics

Local protocol

Monitor:

Respiratory rate

Oxygenation

Ventilation

Mental status

Blood pressure

The AI should never recommend an opioid without considering respiratory depression risk.

26. MORPHINE

Class

Opioid analgesic.

Common indication

Selected moderate-to-severe pain.

Dose is protocol-specific.

Important risks include:

Respiratory depression

Hypotension

Sedation

Nausea/vomiting

Fentanyl and morphine should not be treated as interchangeable medications.

27. MIDAZOLAM

Class

Benzodiazepine.

Common EMS indications

Depending on local protocol:

Seizures

Severe agitation

Procedural sedation

Selected prehospital sedation indications

Dose varies substantially by:

Indication

Route

Age

Weight

Clinical condition

Major risks include:

Respiratory depression

Hypotension

Excessive sedation

The AI must distinguish seizure dosing from sedation/agitation dosing.

28. LORAZEPAM

Class

Benzodiazepine.

Common indication

Seizures and other protocol-defined indications.

Dose and route are protocol-specific.

Monitor:

Respiratory status

Ventilation

Blood pressure

Mental status

29. ADENOSINE

Class

AV nodal antiarrhythmic.

Common indication

Selected regular narrow-complex supraventricular tachycardias.

It is not appropriate for every tachycardia.

Before administration determine:

Rhythm regularity

QRS width

Patient stability

Presence of pre-excitation

Local protocol

The AI should not recommend adenosine for an irregularly irregular rhythm.

Administration should follow the current AHA/local tachycardia algorithm.

The 2025 AHA ALS guidelines specifically address adult tachyarrhythmias including atrial fibrillation/flutter and other life-threatening cardiovascular emergencies.

30. AMIODARONE

Class

Antiarrhythmic.

Common EMS indications

Depending on the protocol:

Certain ventricular tachyarrhythmias

Selected cardiac-arrest rhythms

Other protocol-defined arrhythmias

Dose varies substantially depending on the indication.

The AI must distinguish:

Cardiac arrest

from

Stable wide-complex tachycardia

because dosing and administration are different.

Current AHA ALS guidance includes antiarrhythmic medications in selected cardiac-arrest and tachyarrhythmia situations.

31. LIDOCAINE

Class

Class Ib antiarrhythmic.

Common EMS indication

Selected ventricular arrhythmias.

Use depends on the current protocol and available alternatives.

Dose should be determined from the current cardiac-arrest or tachycardia algorithm.

32. MAGNESIUM SULFATE

Common indications

Protocol-dependent indications include:

Torsades de pointes

Certain severe asthma protocols

Specific electrolyte abnormalities

Selected toxicologic emergencies

Dose depends heavily on the indication.

The AI must never give a generic magnesium dose without first determining the indication.

33. ATROPINE

Class

Antimuscarinic.

Common indication

Selected symptomatic bradycardia.

The current AHA adult bradycardia algorithm should be used for:

Dose

Repeat interval

Maximum dose

Alternative treatments when atropine is ineffective

The AI should distinguish symptomatic bradycardia from an asymptomatic low heart rate.

34. CALCIUM

Calcium may be indicated in selected emergencies such as certain cases of:

Hyperkalemia

Calcium-channel blocker toxicity

Other protocol-defined conditions

Different calcium products have different concentrations and physiologic properties.

Common EMS products include:

Calcium chloride

Calcium gluconate

They should not be treated as equivalent volumes.

The AI must identify the specific calcium preparation before giving a volume.

The 2025 AHA special-circumstances guidelines specifically address hyperkalemia and calcium-channel blocker poisoning.

35. SODIUM BICARBONATE

Sodium bicarbonate is not a routine medication for every cardiac arrest.

Protocol-defined indications can include selected:

Sodium-channel blocker toxicity

Hyperkalemia

Severe metabolic conditions

Other specific toxicologic emergencies

The AI must identify the indication before suggesting administration.

Current AHA ALS guidance specifically discusses bicarbonate and other medications in selected circumstances rather than routine cardiac-arrest use.

36. DOPAMINE

Dopamine may be used in selected shock or bradycardia situations depending on local protocol.

It is generally administered as a continuous infusion.

Dose is commonly expressed as:

mcg/kg/min

The AI must calculate infusion rates using:

Patient weight

Medication concentration

Desired dose

Infusion device

It must not confuse:

mcg/kg/min

with:

mcg/min

37. EPINEPHRINE INFUSION

Epinephrine may be used as a continuous infusion in selected conditions.

Dose is generally expressed as:

mcg/min

or

mcg/kg/min

depending on protocol.

The AI must know:

Patient weight if weight-based

Bag concentration

Desired dose

Pump rate

before calculating mL/hr.

38. FLUIDS

Common prehospital crystalloid solutions include:

0.9% sodium chloride

Lactated Ringer's

Fluid therapy should be individualized.

Potential considerations include:

Hypovolemia

Hemorrhage

Sepsis

Burns

Dehydration

Cardiogenic shock

Renal failure

Heart failure

The AI should avoid recommending large-volume fluid administration without considering the patient's underlying condition.

39. Medication Concentration Safety

The AI should always display both:

Dose

and

Concentration

For example:

Epinephrine
Dose: ___ mg
Concentration: ___ mg/mL
Volume: ___ mL

Never output:

"Give 1 mL epinephrine"

without identifying the concentration.

This is particularly important because epinephrine products may be supplied at different concentrations.

40. Dose Calculation

For liquid medications:

Volume (mL) = Desired dose (mg) ÷ Concentration (mg/mL)

Example:

Desired dose:

5 mg

Available:

2.5 mg/mL

Calculation:

5 ÷ 2.5 = 2 mL

41. Weight-Based Dose Calculation

For a medication ordered as:

0.1 mg/kg

For a:

70-kg patient

Calculation:

0.1 × 70 =

7 mg

If the available concentration is:

1 mg/mL

Then:

7 mg ÷ 1 mg/mL =

7 mL

The AI should display the calculation rather than silently generating a volume.

42. Infusion Calculation

For an infusion:

Dose rate → concentration → mL/hr

The AI should show:

Patient weight

Ordered dose

Medication concentration

Required medication amount per minute

Conversion to mL/hr

Final pump setting

This provides an auditable calculation.

43. Pediatric Medication Safety

Pediatric medication administration requires additional safeguards.

The AI should request:

Age

Weight in kg

Medication

Indication

Concentration

Route

Local pediatric protocol

Never assume the adult dose is appropriate for a child.

Weight should preferably be documented in kilograms.

If weight is unknown, follow the applicable EMS protocol for estimated weight rather than inventing a value.

The 2025 AHA pediatric guidelines specifically include weight-based medication dosing during pediatric resuscitation.

45. Medication Contraindication Logic

The AI should not simply answer:

"Can I give nitroglycerin?"

It should evaluate:

Blood pressure

Suspected diagnosis

PDE-5 inhibitor use

Right ventricular involvement

Other contraindications

Local protocol

Similarly:

"Can I give adenosine?"

should trigger assessment of:

Regularity

QRS width

Stability

Rhythm characteristics

Pre-excitation concerns

Local protocol

The AI should use conditional reasoning rather than treating medications as universally appropriate.

46. Oxygen + Medication Interaction

Oxygen should not automatically be added to every medication protocol.

For example:

Chest pain + normal oxygenation

does not automatically mean:

high-flow oxygen.

Instead evaluate:

SpO2

Respiratory distress

Work of breathing

Clinical appearance

Ventilation

Suspected underlying condition

Oxygen should be titrated according to the patient's condition and applicable protocol.

User

"70 kg adult with severe wheezing, SpO2 88%, speaking in short sentences. What medications?"

AI response structure

Assessment:
Severe respiratory distress with hypoxemia and suspected bronchospasm.

Oxygen:
Provide supplemental oxygen and titrate according to the current EMS protocol and measured oxygenation.

Bronchodilator:
Albuterol — verify the local protocol and stocked concentration before calculating administration.

Additional therapy:
Ipratropium may be indicated in moderate/severe bronchospasm according to protocol.

Monitoring:

SpO2

Respiratory rate

Work of breathing

Mental status

Heart rate

Blood pressure

EtCO2 when available

Escalation:
If ventilation becomes inadequate, prioritize airway/ventilatory support rather than relying solely on oxygen or bronchodilator therapy.

Protocol:
Use the current local EMS asthma/bronchospasm protocol.

49. AI Medication Answer Example With a Dose Calculation

If a protocol states:

Patient:

30 kg

Calculation:

0.01 mg/kg × 30 kg =

0.30 mg

0.1 mg/mL

50. Source Priority

The medication database should use this source hierarchy:

Priority 1

Current local EMS agency protocols and medical-director-approved medication guidelines.

Priority 2

Current state EMS protocols/formulary.

Priority 3

Current AHA/ILCOR recommendations.

The 2025 AHA CPR/ECC guidelines are the current major resuscitation reference and include adult ALS, pediatric life support, special circumstances, and post-cardiac-arrest care.

Priority 4

NAEMSP clinical practice guidance and position statements.

NAEMSP maintains clinical-practice and EMS-medicine guidance resources.

Priority 5

Peer-reviewed literature.

Priority 6

Established pharmacology references.

52. Critical AI Rule

If the AI does not know the applicable protocol or medication concentration, it should say:

"I can provide the general reference information, but I cannot safely determine the administration volume without the medication concentration and applicable EMS protocol."

It should not guess.

53. Final Clinical Safety Principle

The AI should function as:

A protocol-aware reference and calculation assistant.

It should not function as:

An autonomous prescriber.

The preferred decision pathway is:

Patient assessment

↓

Identify clinical problem

↓

Check current local protocol

↓

Confirm indication

↓

Check contraindications

↓

Confirm medication concentration

↓

Calculate dose

↓

Verify dose independently

↓

Administer according to protocol

↓

Reassess patient

↓

Document response
