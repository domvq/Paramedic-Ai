ECG / EKG Interpretation Reference

1. Purpose

An electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) records the electrical activity of the heart over time. ECG interpretation can provide information about:

Heart rate

Cardiac rhythm

Atrial activity

AV conduction

Ventricular conduction

Cardiac axis

Repolarization

Myocardial ischemia or injury patterns

Conduction abnormalities

Certain electrolyte, medication, and toxicologic effects

An ECG should always be interpreted in the context of the patient's clinical presentation, vital signs, history, medications, and previous ECGs when available.

A computerized ECG interpretation is an aid and should not be accepted without clinical review.

2. Standard 12-Lead ECG

A conventional 12-lead ECG uses 10 electrodes to produce 12 electrical views of the heart.

Limb leads

Lead I

Views the heart from the left lateral direction.

Lead II

Views the heart from the inferior direction and commonly provides a useful rhythm strip.

Lead III

Also views the inferior portion of the heart.

aVR

Views the heart from the right shoulder direction.

aVL

Provides a high-lateral view.

aVF

Provides an inferior view.

Precordial leads

V1

Primarily views the right anterior/septal region.

V2

Views the septal/anterior region.

V3

Provides an anterior transition view.

V4

Views the anterior/apical region.

V5

Views the lateral wall.

V6

Views the lateral wall.

Lead placement matters. Incorrect placement can produce misleading ECG morphology and may create apparent abnormalities that are not actually present. Standardization of ECG acquisition and lead placement is addressed in AHA/ACC/HRS recommendations.

3. ECG Calibration

A common standard ECG setting is:

Paper speed: 25 mm/sec

Voltage calibration: 10 mm/mV

At 25 mm/sec:

1 small box = 0.04 seconds

1 large box = 0.20 seconds

5 large boxes = 1 second

At standard calibration:

1 small vertical box = 0.1 mV

10 small vertical boxes = 1 mV

Always verify the displayed paper speed and calibration before calculating intervals or rate.

4. Systematic ECG Interpretation

A systematic approach reduces the chance of missing an important abnormality.

Recommended sequence:

Confirm ECG quality and calibration.

Determine heart rate.

Determine rhythm.

Examine P waves.

Measure PR interval.

Examine QRS complexes.

Determine QRS axis.

Evaluate R-wave progression.

Examine ST segments.

Examine T waves.

Evaluate QT/QTc.

Look for conduction abnormalities.

Look for ischemic/injury patterns.

Compare with previous ECGs when available.

Interpret the ECG in clinical context.

A structured rate-rhythm-axis approach is commonly used in ECG education.

5. Heart Rate

Normal adult resting rate

A typical adult sinus rate is approximately:

60–100 beats/minute

A rate below 60 beats/minute is generally described as bradycardia.

A rate above 100 beats/minute is generally described as tachycardia.

These terms describe rate and do not by themselves establish the underlying rhythm or cause.

6. Calculating Rate

Regular rhythm

At 25 mm/sec:

Rate ≈ 300 ÷ number of large boxes between consecutive R waves

Examples:

1 large box → approximately 300/min

2 large boxes → approximately 150/min

3 large boxes → approximately 100/min

4 large boxes → approximately 75/min

5 large boxes → approximately 60/min

Six-second method

Count QRS complexes occurring during a 6-second interval and multiply by 10.

This method is particularly useful when the rhythm is irregular.

7. Rhythm

Determine whether the rhythm is:

Regular

Regularly irregular

Irregularly irregular

Then evaluate:

Presence of P waves

P-wave morphology

Relationship between P waves and QRS complexes

PR interval

QRS width

Atrial rate versus ventricular rate

A rhythm should not be classified solely by whether it is fast or slow.

8. P Wave

The P wave represents atrial depolarization.

When assessing P waves, determine:

Are P waves present?

Are they occurring before every QRS?

Does every P wave have a QRS?

Are they uniform?

Is their morphology appropriate?

Is there more than one P wave per QRS?

Are P waves hidden within QRS complexes or T waves?

Sinus P-wave pattern

A sinus rhythm generally demonstrates:

Consistent P-wave morphology

P waves preceding QRS complexes

Consistent PR intervals

Appropriate atrial-to-ventricular relationship

Lead II is commonly useful for evaluating P-wave morphology.

9. PR Interval

The PR interval represents conduction from atrial depolarization through the AV node and His-Purkinje system until ventricular depolarization begins.

Typical adult PR interval:

0.12–0.20 seconds

Assess whether the PR interval is:

Normal

Prolonged

Short

Variable

Progressively changing

Completely unrelated to QRS complexes

Abnormal PR relationships can indicate AV conduction abnormalities or pre-excitation.

10. QRS Complex

The QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization.

Evaluate:

Duration

Amplitude

Morphology

Axis

R-wave progression

Presence of pathologic Q waves

Bundle branch block patterns

A narrow QRS generally suggests that ventricular activation is occurring through the normal His-Purkinje conduction system.

A wide QRS can result from:

Ventricular rhythm

Bundle branch block

Aberrant conduction

Pre-excitation

Pacemaker activity

Certain toxicologic or metabolic abnormalities

A wide-complex tachycardia should be approached cautiously because ventricular tachycardia is an important diagnostic consideration.

11. QRS Axis

The QRS axis describes the general direction of ventricular depolarization in the frontal plane.

A commonly used adult reference range is approximately:

−30° to +90°

Possible descriptions include:

Normal axis

Left axis deviation

Right axis deviation

Extreme/northwest axis

Axis determination can provide clues about:

Fascicular block

Chamber enlargement

Conduction disease

Prior myocardial injury

Certain congenital or structural abnormalities

Axis should always be interpreted with the rest of the ECG.

12. R-Wave Progression

Evaluate the progression of the R wave from V1 through V6.

Normally, the R wave generally becomes progressively larger across the precordial leads while the S wave becomes smaller.

Poor R-wave progression may be associated with:

Prior anterior myocardial injury

Lead-placement error

Left ventricular hypertrophy

Conduction abnormalities

Normal variation

Poor R-wave progression is not, by itself, diagnostic of prior myocardial infarction.

13. ST Segment

The ST segment represents the period between ventricular depolarization and repolarization.

Evaluate for:

ST elevation

ST depression

Morphology

Distribution

Contiguous lead involvement

Reciprocal changes

Dynamic changes compared with previous ECGs

ST-segment abnormalities must be interpreted in clinical context.

Possible causes of ST elevation include:

Acute coronary occlusion/injury

Early repolarization

Pericarditis

Left ventricular hypertrophy

Bundle branch block

Ventricular aneurysm

Other structural or metabolic conditions

Possible causes of ST depression include:

Myocardial ischemia

Reciprocal changes

Rate-related changes

LVH with repolarization abnormality

Bundle branch block

Other causes of abnormal repolarization

14. T Wave

The T wave represents ventricular repolarization.

Assess:

Direction

Amplitude

Symmetry

Morphology

Distribution

Relationship to QRS complexes

Abnormal T waves may include:

T-wave inversion

Tall or peaked T waves

Flattened T waves

Biphasic T waves

Hyperacute-appearing T waves

T-wave abnormalities are nonspecific and should be interpreted according to the clinical situation and distribution across leads.

15. QT Interval

The QT interval represents ventricular depolarization and repolarization.

The QT interval varies with heart rate, so a corrected QT interval (QTc) is commonly used.

QT prolongation can increase the risk of certain ventricular arrhythmias, including torsades de pointes.

Potential contributors to prolonged QT include:

Medications

Electrolyte abnormalities

Congenital long-QT syndromes

Bradycardia

Other medical conditions

When evaluating QT, consider both the measured QT and the correction method used by the ECG system.

16. Sinus Bradycardia

Typical characteristics:

Sinus P waves

P wave before each QRS

Consistent PR interval

Usually narrow QRS

Rate below approximately 60/min in adults

Potential causes include:

Normal physiologic state

Increased vagal tone

Athletic conditioning

Inferior myocardial infarction

Hypothermia

Medication effects

Metabolic abnormalities

Conduction system disease

Clinical significance depends heavily on symptoms and hemodynamic status.

17. Sinus Tachycardia

Typical characteristics:

Sinus P waves

P wave before each QRS

Consistent atrial-to-ventricular relationship

Usually narrow QRS

Rate above approximately 100/min

Common causes include:

Exercise

Pain

Anxiety

Fever

Hypovolemia

Hypoxia

Infection

Anemia

Medication effects

Stimulants

Shock

Sinus tachycardia is generally a physiologic response rather than a primary tachyarrhythmia.

The underlying cause should be sought.

18. Atrial Fibrillation

Typical ECG characteristics:

No consistent organized P waves

Irregularly irregular ventricular rhythm

Variable R-R intervals

Fibrillatory baseline may be present

Usually narrow QRS unless another conduction abnormality exists

Atrial fibrillation may be associated with:

Hypertension

Structural heart disease

Heart failure

Valvular disease

Hyperthyroidism

Alcohol or stimulant exposure

Acute illness

Other cardiac conditions

A rapid ventricular response may produce significant symptoms or hemodynamic compromise.

19. Atrial Flutter

Typical characteristics can include:

Organized atrial activity

Flutter waves

Often prominent in inferior leads

Atrial activity frequently occurring around 250–350/min

Variable AV conduction

Common conduction ratios include:

2:1

3:1

4:1

Atrial flutter with 2:1 conduction can produce a ventricular rate near 150/min and may be mistaken for another regular tachycardia.

20. Supraventricular Tachycardia

Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) commonly presents as:

Rapid rhythm

Usually narrow QRS

Often regular

P waves may be hidden within QRS complexes or appear immediately before/after them

Sudden onset and termination may occur

Potential mechanisms include:

AV nodal re-entry

AV re-entry

Atrial tachycardia

The ECG appearance should be interpreted alongside patient presentation and history.

21. Ventricular Tachycardia

Ventricular tachycardia (VT) is a ventricular-origin tachyarrhythmia.

Typical ECG characteristics can include:

Wide QRS complexes

Rapid ventricular rate

AV dissociation

Capture beats

Fusion beats

Extreme axis

Concordance

Other ventricular morphology patterns

Important principle:

A regular wide-complex tachycardia should be treated as VT until an alternative diagnosis has been established by an appropriately qualified clinician.

SVT with aberrant conduction can also produce a wide-complex tachycardia, so morphology and clinical context matter.

22. Ventricular Fibrillation

Ventricular fibrillation is characterized by:

Chaotic ventricular electrical activity

No organized QRS complexes

No effective coordinated ventricular contraction

VF is a cardiac arrest rhythm and requires immediate recognition within the applicable resuscitation protocol.

23. AV Conduction Blocks

First-Degree AV Block

Typical ECG finding:

PR interval > 0.20 seconds

Every atrial impulse is still conducted to the ventricles.

Second-Degree AV Block — Mobitz I

Also called Wenckebach.

Typical pattern:

Progressive PR prolongation

Then a nonconducted P wave

Pattern repeats

The ventricular rhythm may be irregular.

Second-Degree AV Block — Mobitz II

Typical pattern:

Constant PR intervals in conducted beats

Intermittent nonconducted P waves

Sudden dropped QRS complexes

This is a clinically important conduction abnormality and may progress to more advanced AV block.

Third-Degree AV Block

Also called complete heart block.

Typical characteristics:

Atrial activity continues independently

Ventricular activity continues independently

No consistent PR relationship

AV dissociation

The ventricular escape rhythm may be narrow or wide depending on the origin of the escape rhythm.

24. Bundle Branch Blocks

Right Bundle Branch Block

Typical features may include:

Prolonged QRS

Right-precordial terminal conduction abnormalities

Characteristic QRS morphology in V1/V2

Broad terminal S waves in lateral leads

Left Bundle Branch Block

Typical features may include:

Prolonged QRS

Abnormal septal depolarization

Broad/notched R waves in lateral leads

Secondary ST-T abnormalities

Bundle branch block patterns can complicate assessment of ischemia.

25. ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction / Acute Coronary Occlusion

When evaluating a patient with possible acute coronary syndrome:

Obtain and interpret a 12-lead ECG promptly.

Assess symptoms and clinical presentation.

Examine contiguous leads.

Look for reciprocal changes.

Compare with previous ECGs when available.

Look for dynamic ECG changes.

Consider posterior or right-sided involvement when clinically indicated.

Follow applicable local STEMI/ACS protocols.

AHA/ACC/HRS standards recognize the ECG as an important tool in the evaluation of acute coronary syndromes.

The ECG should not be interpreted in isolation from the patient's symptoms and clinical condition.

26. Inferior Lead Group

Inferior leads:

II

III

aVF

These leads primarily view the inferior portion of the heart.

When inferior ischemic/injury patterns are suspected, evaluate:

ST elevation/depression

Reciprocal changes

Rhythm

AV conduction

Right-sided involvement when clinically appropriate

27. Anterior Lead Group

Anterior precordial leads generally include:

V1

V2

V3

V4

Abnormalities in these leads may indicate anterior/septal pathology but should be interpreted using the complete ECG pattern.

28. Lateral Lead Group

Lateral leads include:

I

aVL

V5

V6

Evaluate these leads together when assessing lateral ischemic or injury patterns.

29. Posterior Myocardial Injury

The posterior wall is not directly viewed by the standard 12-lead ECG.

Posterior involvement may produce reciprocal changes in the anterior leads.

When clinically appropriate, additional posterior leads may be obtained, commonly:

V7

V8

V9

The interpretation should follow local protocol and the clinical context.

30. Right-Sided ECG

Right-sided precordial leads may be useful when right ventricular involvement is suspected, particularly in appropriate inferior myocardial infarction presentations.

V4R is commonly used to assess the right ventricle.

Additional leads may be obtained according to local protocol.

31. Electrolyte and Metabolic Patterns

ECG abnormalities can occur with electrolyte disturbances.

Examples include:

Hyperkalemia

Possible findings can include:

Tall/peaked T waves

PR prolongation

QRS widening

P-wave abnormalities

Progressive conduction abnormalities

Severe cases may progress toward a sine-wave appearance

ECG findings vary and a normal ECG does not reliably exclude hyperkalemia.

Hypokalemia

Possible findings can include:

Flattened T waves

ST depression

Prominent U waves

Apparent QT/QU prolongation

ECG findings should be interpreted with laboratory results when available.

32. ECG Artifacts

Artifacts can mimic clinically important ECG abnormalities.

Potential sources include:

Patient movement

Muscle tremor

Poor electrode contact

Electrical interference

Loose cables

Incorrect lead placement

Equipment problems

Before interpreting an unusual ECG, verify that the tracing is technically adequate.

If the ECG does not fit the patient's clinical presentation, repeat the ECG after correcting possible technical problems.

33. Common ECG Interpretation Errors

Avoid:

Relying exclusively on the computerized interpretation

Ignoring lead placement

Ignoring calibration

Calling every wide-complex tachycardia SVT

Calling every ST elevation STEMI

Ignoring reciprocal changes

Ignoring the clinical presentation

Failing to compare with previous ECGs

Interpreting a single lead without reviewing the entire tracing

Assuming an abnormal ECG automatically identifies the patient's diagnosis

34. Recommended AI Response Structure

When the Paramedic AI is given an ECG description, it should organize its response as:

1. Rate

State the approximate ventricular rate.

2. Rhythm

State whether the rhythm appears regular, irregular, or irregularly irregular and describe the likely rhythm.

3. P waves

Describe presence, morphology, and relationship to QRS complexes.

4. PR interval

State whether it appears normal, prolonged, shortened, or variable when measurable.

5. QRS

Describe width and morphology.

6. Axis

State the apparent QRS axis when sufficient information is available.

7. ST segments

Describe significant ST elevation/depression and its distribution.

8. T waves

Describe significant T-wave abnormalities.

9. QT/QTc

Describe whether QT/QTc appears prolonged when measurable.

10. Overall interpretation

Provide a concise interpretation based only on the available ECG information.

11. Clinical correlation

Identify important clinical considerations without presenting the ECG interpretation as a definitive diagnosis.

12. Urgency

If the described ECG contains a potentially life-threatening pattern, explicitly state that the finding warrants immediate clinical assessment according to the applicable EMS/local protocol.

35. AI Safety Rules

The Paramedic AI should follow these rules:

Do not claim certainty when the ECG description is incomplete.

Do not invent ECG measurements that were not provided.

Do not infer a diagnosis solely from heart rate.

Do not rely solely on automated ECG interpretation.

Distinguish ECG findings from clinical diagnoses.

State when additional leads or a repeat ECG may be clinically relevant.

Encourage comparison with prior ECGs when available.

Follow the user's local EMS protocols when those protocols are provided to the system.

Do not provide treatment instructions that conflict with local protocols.

For unstable or potentially life-threatening presentations, prioritize immediate clinical assessment and established emergency protocols.

Clearly identify uncertainty.

Cite the underlying documentation used to produce the answer whenever possible.

When to Obtain and How to Interpret a 12-Lead ECG

1. Purpose

A 12-lead ECG is a rapid diagnostic tool used to evaluate cardiac electrical activity, identify dysrhythmias and conduction abnormalities, and recognize patterns that may indicate myocardial ischemia or injury.

An ECG should not be interpreted in isolation. Interpretation should be integrated with:

Patient history

Presenting complaint

Vital signs

Physical examination

Cardiac monitoring

Previous ECGs

Laboratory testing when available

Local EMS protocols

Medical direction

The ECG is a diagnostic test, not a diagnosis by itself.

2. When Should a 12-Lead ECG Be Obtained?

A 12-lead ECG should be considered whenever the patient has symptoms or findings that could represent cardiac disease.

Strong indications

Obtain a 12-lead ECG when the patient has:

Chest pain or pressure

Chest discomfort

Epigastric discomfort potentially representing an anginal equivalent

Unexplained dyspnea

Syncope or near-syncope

Palpitations

Unexplained weakness or diaphoresis

Suspected acute coronary syndrome

Suspected dysrhythmia

Bradycardia

Tachycardia

New irregular pulse

Suspected heart failure

Suspected myocardial ischemia

Hemodynamic instability potentially related to a cardiac cause

A significant change in cardiac status

Chest pain should not be interpreted narrowly. Anginal-equivalent symptoms can include discomfort in the shoulders, arms, neck, back, upper abdomen, or jaw, as well as shortness of breath and fatigue.

3. Acute Chest Pain

In a patient presenting with acute chest pain or suspected ACS:

Obtain and review the 12-lead ECG as early as possible.

Current AHA/ACC guidance recommends that an ECG be acquired and reviewed for STEMI within 10 minutes of arrival in patients with acute chest pain.

For EMS systems, local protocols may specify an even more aggressive prehospital timeline.

The ECG should be interpreted immediately rather than simply transmitted and left for later review when the patient's condition or presentation suggests a time-sensitive cardiac emergency.

4. Do Not Stop After a Normal ECG

A normal initial ECG does not rule out acute coronary syndrome.

If:

Symptoms continue,

Symptoms recur,

The patient's condition changes, or

Clinical suspicion for ACS remains high,

additional ECGs should be considered according to the applicable protocol.

Serial ECGs can reveal dynamic ischemic changes that were absent on the initial tracing. AHA/ACC guidance specifically notes that an initial normal or nondiagnostic ECG is not sufficient by itself to exclude ACS.

5. Other Situations Requiring ECG Evaluation

Bradycardia

A 12-lead ECG can help determine:

Sinus versus non-sinus rhythm

AV block

Escape rhythm

QRS width

Conduction abnormalities

QT abnormalities

Possible metabolic or medication-related abnormalities

A resting ECG is an important component of the evaluation of suspected bradycardia or conduction disease.

Tachycardia

Obtain a 12-lead ECG when possible to determine:

Narrow versus wide QRS

Regular versus irregular rhythm

Atrial versus ventricular activity

Possible SVT

Atrial fibrillation

Atrial flutter

Ventricular tachycardia

Pre-excitation

Other conduction abnormalities

If the patient is unstable, treatment and resuscitation should not be delayed merely to obtain a perfect 12-lead ECG.

Syncope or Near-Syncope

Consider a 12-lead ECG when evaluating unexplained syncope or near-syncope.

Look particularly for:

Bradyarrhythmia

AV block

QT prolongation

Pre-excitation

Brugada-pattern abnormalities

Significant conduction disease

Ventricular arrhythmia patterns

Evidence of prior myocardial injury

The ECG is one component of the overall syncope evaluation.

Palpitations

A 12-lead ECG can help determine whether symptoms are associated with:

Sinus tachycardia

Atrial fibrillation

Atrial flutter

SVT

PVCs

Ventricular tachycardia

Pre-excitation

Other conduction abnormalities

If symptoms are intermittent, the ECG obtained between episodes may be normal.

6. Obtain the ECG Correctly

Before interpretation, verify:

Patient identification

Confirm:

Correct patient

Correct time/date

Correct ECG

Appropriate clinical information

Lead placement

Ensure electrodes are placed correctly.

Incorrect lead placement can produce significant changes in:

QRS morphology

R-wave progression

Axis

ST segments

T waves

If an ECG looks inconsistent with the patient's presentation, consider repeating it after verifying electrode placement.

Calibration

Verify:

Paper/display speed

Gain/calibration

Filter settings when relevant

Do not calculate intervals or rate without knowing the recording settings.

7. The Systematic ECG Interpretation Sequence

The AI should use a consistent interpretation sequence.

Step 1 — Check the ECG quality

Ask:

Is there significant artifact?

Are all leads present?

Is lead placement plausible?

Is calibration standard?

Is the tracing interpretable?

Do not attempt a detailed interpretation of a technically inadequate ECG without acknowledging the limitation.

Step 2 — Determine the Rate

Determine:

Atrial rate when identifiable

Ventricular rate

For a regular rhythm at 25 mm/sec:

Rate ≈ 300 ÷ number of large boxes between R waves

For an irregular rhythm:

Use an appropriate timed-strip method or the ECG machine's rate while recognizing its limitations.

8. Step 3 — Determine the Rhythm

Ask:

Is the rhythm regular?

Is it irregular?

Is it irregularly irregular?

Are P waves present?

Does every P wave precede a QRS?

Does every QRS have a preceding P wave?

Is the PR relationship consistent?

Is there AV dissociation?

Possible rhythm categories include:

Sinus rhythm

Sinus bradycardia

Sinus tachycardia

Atrial fibrillation

Atrial flutter

SVT

Ventricular tachycardia

Junctional rhythm

AV block

Paced rhythm

9. Step 4 — Evaluate P Waves

Determine:

Present or absent

Morphology

Regularity

Relationship to QRS

Number of P waves per QRS

Ask:

Are the P waves sinus in appearance?

If P waves are absent, consider:

Atrial fibrillation

Junctional rhythm

Fine flutter activity

Artifact

Other possibilities

Do not automatically diagnose atrial fibrillation solely because P waves are difficult to see.

10. Step 5 — Measure the PR Interval

Evaluate whether the PR interval is:

Normal

Prolonged

Short

Variable

Progressively increasing

Unrelated to QRS complexes

A typical adult PR interval is approximately:

0.12–0.20 seconds

Important patterns include:

Prolonged PR

Consider first-degree AV block.

Progressive PR prolongation followed by a dropped QRS

Consider Mobitz I/Wenckebach.

Constant PR intervals with intermittent dropped QRS complexes

Consider Mobitz II.

No consistent relationship between P waves and QRS complexes

Consider complete AV block.

11. Step 6 — Evaluate the QRS

Determine:

Narrow or wide

Duration

Morphology

Bundle branch pattern

R-wave progression

Pathologic Q waves

Capture/fusion beats when evaluating wide-complex tachycardia

A wide QRS may result from:

Ventricular rhythm

Bundle branch block

Aberrant conduction

Pre-excitation

Pacemaker activity

Metabolic/toxicologic causes

A wide-complex tachycardia should be approached cautiously.

When the mechanism is uncertain, ventricular tachycardia is an important consideration.

12. Step 7 — Determine Axis

Evaluate the frontal-plane QRS axis.

Describe it as:

Normal axis

Left axis deviation

Right axis deviation

Extreme axis

Axis abnormalities can provide clues regarding:

Fascicular block

Chamber enlargement

Conduction disease

Prior myocardial injury

Structural disease

Axis is supportive information and should not be interpreted independently.

13. Step 8 — Evaluate R-Wave Progression

Review V1 through V6.

Assess whether the R wave progresses appropriately across the precordial leads.

Poor R-wave progression can result from:

Lead-placement error

Prior anterior myocardial injury

LVH

Conduction abnormalities

Normal variation

Do not diagnose previous myocardial infarction solely because R-wave progression is poor.

14. Step 9 — Evaluate the ST Segments

Evaluate the ST segment in every lead.

Look for:

ST elevation

ST depression

Horizontal changes

Downsloping changes

Reciprocal changes

Distribution across contiguous leads

Dynamic changes compared with previous ECGs

Never interpret ST elevation from one lead in isolation.

Ask:

Where are the changes?

Are they in anatomically related leads?

Are reciprocal changes present?

Does the clinical presentation support acute coronary occlusion?

ST elevation has multiple causes and does not automatically equal STEMI.

15. Step 10 — Evaluate T Waves

Look for:

T-wave inversion

Tall/peaked T waves

Hyperacute-appearing T waves

Flattened T waves

Biphasic T waves

Symmetry

Distribution

T-wave abnormalities should be interpreted in the context of the QRS, ST segment, symptoms, and lead distribution.

16. Step 11 — Evaluate QT/QTc

Evaluate the QT interval and corrected QT.

Potential causes of prolonged QT include:

Medications

Electrolyte abnormalities

Congenital long-QT syndromes

Bradycardia

Other medical conditions

Marked QT prolongation can increase the risk of certain ventricular arrhythmias.

17. Step 12 — Look for Ischemia and Injury Patterns

After evaluating the individual components, step back and examine the ECG as a whole.

Ask:

Are there ST changes?

Are the changes in contiguous leads?

Are reciprocal changes present?

Are there hyperacute T waves?

Is there a new conduction abnormality?

Is there a new Q-wave pattern?

Does the ECG differ from a previous tracing?

Does the ECG fit the patient's symptoms?

18. Compare With Previous ECGs

Whenever a previous ECG is available, compare it.

Look for:

New ST changes

New T-wave changes

New Q waves

New conduction abnormalities

New axis changes

New rhythm

Changes in QT

Changes in QRS morphology

A previously abnormal ECG can be much less concerning if the abnormality is known to be chronic.

Conversely, a previously normal ECG becoming abnormal can be highly significant.

19. Consider Additional Leads

A standard 12-lead ECG does not directly visualize every region of the heart equally.

When clinically appropriate, additional leads may be useful.

Right-sided leads

Consider when right ventricular involvement is suspected.

V4R is commonly used.

Posterior leads

Consider when posterior myocardial involvement is suspected.

Common additional leads:

V7

V8

V9

A normal standard 12-lead ECG does not exclude all coronary occlusions, including some posterior or circumflex-territory events.

Follow local EMS protocols for when and how additional leads are obtained.

20. Computerized ECG Interpretation

The computer interpretation should be treated as:

Decision support — not the final diagnosis.

The clinician should independently review:

Rate

Rhythm

Intervals

QRS

ST segments

T waves

Overall morphology

Computer algorithms can miss clinically important abnormalities and can also generate false-positive interpretations.

A computer-generated interpretation should never replace clinical assessment.

21. A Practical 60-Second ECG Method

For rapid field interpretation, use:

RATE

What is the ventricular rate?

RHYTHM

Regular or irregular?

P WAVES

Present? Sinus? Relationship to QRS?

PR

Normal, prolonged, shortened, or variable?

QRS

Narrow or wide?

AXIS

Normal, left, right, or extreme?

R-WAVE PROGRESSION

Normal or abnormal?

ST

Elevation? Depression? Reciprocal changes?

T WAVES

Normal? Inverted? Hyperacute? Peaked?

QT

Normal or prolonged?

COMPARE

What does the previous ECG show?

CONTEXT

Does the ECG fit the patient's presentation?

22. ECG Red Flags

The AI should immediately flag potentially high-risk patterns such as:

Ventricular fibrillation

Sustained ventricular tachycardia

Significant symptomatic bradycardia

High-grade AV block

Complete heart block

Significant ST-segment elevation/injury pattern

Significant ST depression with concerning symptoms

New ischemic changes

Dangerous wide-complex tachycardia

Markedly prolonged QT with concerning rhythm

Significant hyperkalemia-associated ECG changes

New concerning conduction abnormalities

ECG abnormalities associated with hemodynamic instability

The AI should not independently declare that a patient is having a specific life-threatening diagnosis solely from an ECG description when the available information is insufficient.

25. Important AI Safety Rules

The AI should:

Never invent an ECG measurement.

Never claim to see a waveform that was not supplied.

Distinguish between an ECG finding and a clinical diagnosis.

State uncertainty when the description is incomplete.

Never assume the computerized interpretation is correct.

Never use one ECG finding in isolation.

Compare with previous ECGs when available.

Consider lead placement and artifact.

Consider the clinical presentation.

Follow the user's current local EMS protocol when available.

Identify potentially life-threatening patterns promptly.

Never delay emergency assessment because additional ECG interpretation is needed.

Recommend repeat/serial ECGs when clinically appropriate rather than treating one normal ECG as definitive exclusion of ACS.

Preserve the source used for the interpretation.

Clearly distinguish educational information from protocol-directed patient care.

26. Core Principle

The correct way to interpret an ECG is not:

"What diagnosis does this ECG show?"

Instead ask:

"What does the ECG show, where are the abnormalities, how do those findings fit together, and do they fit the patient's clinical presentation?"

A systematic interpretation should move from:

Technical quality → Rate → Rhythm → P waves → PR → QRS → Axis → R-wave progression → ST segments → T waves → QT/QTc → comparison → clinical context.

The ECG is one component of patient assessment. It should be interpreted together with the history, physical examination, vital signs, monitoring, previous ECGs, and other diagnostic information.
