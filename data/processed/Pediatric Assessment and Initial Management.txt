Pediatric Assessment and Initial Management

1. Purpose

This document provides a structured framework for the prehospital assessment and initial management of pediatric patients.

It is intended to support paramedics and a documentation-grounded paramedic AI in recognizing pediatric illness, identifying deterioration, selecting appropriate assessment priorities, and locating the appropriate pediatric protocol.

Current local EMS protocols, medical-director orders, pediatric medication protocols, scope of practice, manufacturer instructions, and applicable law take precedence over this general reference.

The 2025 American Heart Association (AHA) and American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) pediatric guidelines address pediatric patients in prearrest, intra-arrest, and post-arrest states and include pediatric BLS and advanced life support.

2. Pediatric Age Definitions

The AI should identify the patient's developmental category before providing pediatric-specific recommendations.

For 2025 AHA pediatric BLS guidance:

Infant

Approximately younger than 1 year of age, excluding newborn infants.

Child

Approximately 1 year of age until puberty.

Puberty and older

Patients showing signs of puberty should generally follow adult BLS guidance.

The pediatric BLS guidelines specifically exclude newborn infants, whose resuscitation follows neonatal guidance because of their unique transition from fetal to neonatal physiology.

Local EMS definitions may differ and should take precedence.

3. Pediatric Assessment Principles

Pediatric assessment should rapidly determine:

Is the child critically ill?

Is the airway patent?

Is breathing adequate?

Is oxygenation adequate?

Is ventilation adequate?

Is circulation adequate?

Is the child in shock?

Is the child at risk for respiratory failure?

Is the child experiencing cardiac arrest?

What is the most likely underlying cause?

Pediatric patients may compensate for significant illness before suddenly deteriorating.

Early recognition of respiratory failure and shock is therefore important.

4. Pediatric Appearance

Speech or cry

A markedly abnormal appearance may indicate serious illness even before severe vital-sign abnormalities develop.

7. Pediatric Ventilation

If a pediatric patient has inadequate or absent breathing, ventilation support should be initiated promptly.

The 2025 AHA pediatric BLS guidelines emphasize rapid support of ventilation and oxygenation in seriously ill children.

Potential interventions include:

Appropriate airway positioning

Supplemental oxygen when indicated

Bag-valve-mask ventilation

Advanced airway management when indicated

Continuous reassessment

Avoid both inadequate and excessive ventilation.

8. Pediatric Oxygenation

Assess oxygenation using:

Clinical appearance

Pulse oximetry

Work of breathing

Skin color

Mental status

Oxygen therapy should follow the applicable pediatric respiratory protocol.

Do not use pulse oximetry as the sole indicator of respiratory status.

A child can have significant respiratory distress before oxygen saturation becomes severely abnormal.

9. Pediatric Circulation

Assess:

Heart rate

Pulse quality

Blood pressure when appropriate

Capillary refill when useful

Skin temperature

Skin color

Mental status

Urine output when history is available

Signs of shock

Pediatric patients can maintain blood pressure despite significant circulatory compromise.

Therefore, normal blood pressure does not automatically exclude shock.

10. Pediatric Shock

Consider shock when there is evidence of inadequate tissue perfusion.

Potential findings include:

Tachycardia

Weak peripheral pulses

Delayed capillary refill

Cool extremities

Pale or mottled skin

Altered mental status

Decreased urine output

Hypotension

Increasing respiratory distress

Abnormal skin temperature

Potential causes include:

Hypovolemic shock

Examples:

Hemorrhage

Dehydration

Vomiting

Diarrhea

Burns

Distributive shock

Examples:

Sepsis

Anaphylaxis

Cardiogenic shock

Examples:

Myocarditis

Cardiomyopathy

Congenital heart disease

Dysrhythmia

Obstructive shock

Examples:

Tension pneumothorax

Cardiac tamponade

Pulmonary embolism

Treatment should follow the appropriate pediatric shock protocol and underlying cause.

The 2025 PALS guidelines specifically address pediatric shock and different shock etiologies.

12. Pediatric Heart Rate

Evaluate:

Rate

Rhythm

Regularity

Pulse quality

Relationship to clinical condition

Perfusion

Physiologic

Related to fever

Related to pain

Related to dehydration

Related to hypoxia

Related to shock

Caused by a primary arrhythmia

Bradycardia in a seriously ill child may indicate significant hypoxia or respiratory failure.

13. Pediatric Cardiac Arrest

Pediatric cardiac arrest is frequently associated with respiratory failure or shock rather than an isolated primary cardiac event.

When cardiac arrest is recognized:

Begin high-quality CPR.

Provide effective ventilation.

Apply the monitor/defibrillator.

Identify the rhythm.

Defibrillate shockable rhythms when indicated.

Establish vascular access.

Administer appropriate medications according to current pediatric protocol.

Identify and treat reversible causes.

Reassess continuously.

The 2025 pediatric BLS guidance emphasizes conventional CPR with both compressions and ventilations for infants and children in cardiac arrest.

14. Pediatric CPR

For pediatric cardiac arrest, use current pediatric resuscitation guidance.

The 2025 AHA/AAP pediatric BLS guidelines recommend:

Compression rate of approximately 100–120/min

Compression depth of approximately one-third of the anterior-posterior chest diameter

Approximately 4 cm for infants

Approximately 5 cm for children

Minimizing interruptions

Complete chest recoil

For infants, the 2025 guidelines no longer recommend the 2-finger compression technique because of concerns about achieving adequate depth; 1-hand or 2-thumb–encircling-hands techniques are recommended.

15. Pediatric AED/Defibrillation

Use an AED or manual defibrillator as soon as available during pediatric cardiac arrest.

When available, a pediatric attenuator may be used according to current guidance and equipment instructions.

The 2025 AHA/AAP pediatric BLS guidelines recommend immediate AED application/use for pediatric cardiac arrest when available, including use of a pediatric attenuator when available.

Defibrillation energy should come from the current pediatric resuscitation protocol and device-specific guidance.

Cardiac disease

Treatment should follow the current pediatric bradycardia algorithm.

18. Pediatric Tachycardia

Determine whether tachycardia is likely:

Sinus tachycardia

Supraventricular tachycardia

Another atrial rhythm

Ventricular tachycardia

Secondary to another illness

Assess:

Heart rate

Rhythm regularity

QRS width

P waves

Clinical symptoms

Perfusion

Blood pressure

Underlying illness

A fast heart rate in a sick child should not automatically be labeled SVT.

Pain, fever, hypoxia, dehydration, and shock can all produce sinus tachycardia.

20. Foreign-Body Airway Obstruction

Assess whether the obstruction is:

Mild/partial

Severe/complete

The 2025 pediatric BLS guidelines provide different interventions for infants and children with severe foreign-body airway obstruction.

For infants with severe obstruction, the current guideline uses repeated cycles of 5 back blows followed by 5 chest thrusts.

For children with severe obstruction, the guideline uses repeated cycles of 5 back blows followed by 5 abdominal thrusts.

Blind finger sweeps should not be performed.

If the patient becomes unresponsive, initiate CPR according to the current pediatric algorithm and remove a visible foreign body when opening the airway.

34. Weight

When medication dosing or fluid therapy is weight-dependent, obtain an actual measured weight when feasible.

If an actual weight is unavailable:

Follow the local EMS-approved weight-estimation method.

Clearly identify that the weight is estimated.

Do not present an estimated weight as an actual weight.

The AI should distinguish:

Actual weight

from

Estimated weight

and should preserve the source of the weight in its response.

Newborn resuscitation should have a separate folder based on current neonatal resuscitation guidance.

Maximum doses

Defibrillation energies

Cardioversion energies

Fluid boluses

Equipment sizes

Vital-sign thresholds

Scope-of-practice rules

Pediatric destination requirements

If the required information is unavailable, the AI should explicitly say so.

Example:

"The provided pediatric protocol does not specify the dose for this indication. I cannot reliably determine the protocol-specific dose from the supplied documentation."

45. Conflicting Pediatric Documents

If two pediatric documents conflict:

Identify both documents.

Check effective dates.

Determine whether they apply to different age groups.

Determine whether they apply to different indications.

Determine whether one is an agency protocol and the other is general guidance.

Prefer the current authoritative local protocol.

Flag unresolved conflicts.

The AI should never silently choose one conflicting pediatric dose or treatment recommendation.

48. Final AI Instruction

When answering pediatric questions, prioritize:

Current local pediatric protocol → current pediatric medication/formulary documentation → current AHA/AAP/PALS guidance → manufacturer information → other authoritative pediatric references → general medical knowledge.

The AI should identify uncertainty whenever documentation is incomplete.

Document classification: General prehospital pediatric reference

Protocol authority: Local EMS agency / medical director

Primary guideline reference: 2025 AHA/AAP Pediatric Basic Life Support and Pediatric Advanced Life Support guidelines

Newborn care: Separate neonatal reference required

Review requirement: Verify all pediatric clinical information against current local protocols before clinical use.
