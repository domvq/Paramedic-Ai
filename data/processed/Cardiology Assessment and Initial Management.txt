Cardiology Assessment and Initial Management

1. Purpose

This document provides a structured framework for the prehospital assessment and initial management of patients with suspected cardiovascular disease or cardiovascular instability.

It is intended as a general clinical-reference document for paramedics and as a knowledge source for a documentation-grounded paramedic AI.

Current local EMS protocols, medical-director orders, scope of practice, medication protocols, and applicable law take precedence over this document.

The 2025 American Heart Association (AHA) Guidelines for CPR and Emergency Cardiovascular Care provide current evidence-based guidance for adult basic life support, adult advanced life support, special circumstances of resuscitation, and post-cardiac-arrest care.

2. Initial Cardiovascular Assessment

The initial cardiovascular assessment should rapidly determine:

Is the patient in cardiac arrest?

Is there a pulse?

Is the patient hemodynamically stable?

Is there evidence of acute coronary syndrome?

Is there a significant arrhythmia?

Is there evidence of heart failure or cardiogenic shock?

Is there another condition causing cardiovascular instability?

Does the patient require immediate intervention?

Assessment should occur simultaneously with treatment when the patient is unstable.

3. Primary Cardiovascular Assessment

Assess:

Level of consciousness

Airway

Breathing

Circulation

Central and peripheral pulses

Heart rate

Blood pressure

Skin temperature and appearance

Skin color

Capillary refill when clinically useful

Respiratory effort

Oxygen saturation

Cardiac rhythm

12-lead ECG when indicated

Blood glucose when clinically indicated

End-tidal carbon dioxide when indicated

Signs of poor perfusion

Potential signs of cardiovascular instability include:

Altered mental status

Hypotension

Severe hypertension associated with acute illness

Chest pain or pressure

Severe dyspnea

Syncope

Weak or absent peripheral pulses

Cool, pale, or diaphoretic skin

Pulmonary edema

Cyanosis

Signs of shock

Severe or symptomatic bradycardia

Severe or symptomatic tachycardia

The 2025 AHA Adult Advanced Life Support guidelines emphasize rapid assessment of clinical stability to determine appropriate treatment in patients with life-threatening cardiovascular emergencies.

4. Cardiac Arrest Recognition

Cardiac arrest should be suspected in an unresponsive patient with absent or abnormal breathing and no definite pulse.

Immediate priorities include:

Recognition of cardiac arrest

Activation of the appropriate response system

High-quality CPR

Rapid rhythm assessment

Defibrillation when indicated

Appropriate airway and ventilation management

Vascular access

Reversible-cause assessment

Ongoing reassessment

The 2025 AHA Adult BLS guidelines address recognition of cardiac arrest, high-quality CPR, and AED use.

5. Cardiac Arrest Rhythms

Cardiac arrest rhythms are broadly divided into:

Shockable rhythms

Ventricular fibrillation (VF)

Pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT)

Nonshockable rhythms

Asystole

Pulseless electrical activity (PEA)

Treatment should follow the current cardiac-arrest algorithm and applicable local protocol.

The 2025 AHA Adult Advanced Life Support guidelines specifically address VF, pVT, asystole, PEA, defibrillation, medications, vascular access, airway management, and other advanced resuscitation interventions.

6. High-Quality CPR

When cardiac arrest is confirmed:

Begin CPR promptly.

Minimize interruptions in chest compressions.

Maintain appropriate compression quality.

Allow appropriate chest recoil.

Avoid unnecessary pauses.

Use a defibrillator/AED as soon as available.

Coordinate rhythm assessment and defibrillation with minimal interruption.

Provide ventilation appropriate to the patient's airway and resuscitation phase.

Continuously reassess for ROSC.

Mechanical CPR, when available, should be used according to local protocol and system-specific indications.

The 2025 AHA guidelines emphasize high-quality CPR as a core component of cardiac-arrest management.

7. Defibrillation

Defibrillation is indicated for appropriate shockable cardiac-arrest rhythms.

Key principles include:

Rapid recognition of shockable rhythm

Prompt defibrillation when indicated

Appropriate pad placement

Safe charging and shock delivery

Immediate resumption of CPR after shock when indicated

Rhythm reassessment according to the resuscitation algorithm

Defibrillation energy settings must follow the specific defibrillator manufacturer's instructions and current local protocol.

The 2025 AHA guidelines provide updated recommendations for defibrillation and advanced life support.

8. Return of Spontaneous Circulation

ROSC should be suspected when there is evidence of restored circulation.

Potential findings include:

Palpable pulse

Abrupt increase in end-tidal CO₂

Spontaneous arterial pressure

Improvement in skin perfusion

Return of consciousness

Spontaneous movement

Organized cardiac activity associated with circulation

ROSC should be confirmed clinically and with appropriate monitoring.

A patient who achieves ROSC requires immediate transition from cardiac-arrest management to post-cardiac-arrest care.

9. Post-Cardiac Arrest Care

Post-cardiac-arrest care includes management of:

Airway

Oxygenation

Ventilation

Blood pressure

Cardiac rhythm

Neurologic status

Glucose

Temperature

Potential coronary occlusion

Reversible causes

Transport to an appropriate receiving facility

The 2025 AHA post-cardiac-arrest guidelines address oxygen, ventilation, blood pressure, glucose, temperature control, diagnostic testing, coronary intervention, and other components of care after ROSC.

Patients with a pulse should be assessed for:

Rate

Rhythm

Regularity

QRS width

P-wave relationship

Symptoms

Hemodynamic stability

Underlying cause

A rhythm alone should not determine treatment.

Determine whether the rhythm is:

Slow

Normal rate

Fast

Then determine whether the patient is:

Stable

Unstable

11. Bradycardia

Bradycardia is a slow heart rate that may or may not produce clinical instability.

Assess for:

Hypotension

Altered mental status

Syncope or near-syncope

Signs of shock

Ischemic chest discomfort

Acute heart failure

Poor perfusion

Potential causes include:

Medication effects

Hypoxia

Acute myocardial infarction

Increased vagal tone

Metabolic abnormalities

Electrolyte abnormalities

Conduction-system disease

Hypothermia

Treatment should be based on the patient's clinical condition and applicable bradycardia algorithm.

The 2025 AHA Adult Advanced Life Support guidelines include an adult bradycardia-with-pulse algorithm.

12. Tachycardia

Tachycardia should be evaluated in the context of the patient's clinical condition.

Determine:

Heart rate

Regularity

QRS width

P-wave characteristics

Duration

Symptoms

Hemodynamic stability

Possible underlying cause

Potential causes include:

Hypovolemia

Hypoxia

Infection

Fever

Pain

Anxiety

Metabolic abnormalities

Drug effects

Acute coronary syndrome

Pulmonary embolism

Primary cardiac arrhythmia

A rapid rhythm may be either the primary problem or a physiologic response to another illness.

13. Unstable Tachyarrhythmia

Signs of clinically significant instability may include:

Hypotension

Altered mental status

Shock

Ischemic chest discomfort

Acute pulmonary edema or heart failure

When an unstable tachyarrhythmia is identified, treatment should follow the current tachyarrhythmia algorithm and local protocol.

The 2025 AHA ALS guidelines include recommendations for tachyarrhythmias and electrical cardioversion.

14. 12-Lead ECG

A 12-lead ECG is an important diagnostic tool for patients with suspected acute coronary syndrome and other cardiovascular conditions.

When indicated, assess:

Rate

Rhythm

P waves

PR interval

QRS duration

QT/QTc

ST-segment changes

T-wave changes

Q waves

Axis

Conduction abnormalities

Compare with previous ECGs when available.

The ECG should be interpreted in the context of:

Patient symptoms

Physical examination

Medical history

Vital signs

Time course

Previous ECGs

Other diagnostic information

15. Acute Coronary Syndrome

Suspect acute coronary syndrome (ACS) when a patient presents with symptoms potentially caused by myocardial ischemia.

Possible symptoms include:

Chest pressure or discomfort

Chest pain

Dyspnea

Diaphoresis

Nausea or vomiting

Syncope

Upper-extremity discomfort

Jaw, neck, shoulder, or back discomfort

Unexplained weakness

ACS can occur without classic chest pain.

Risk assessment should consider patient history and associated symptoms rather than relying on a single symptom.

16. ECG Findings Suggestive of Acute Coronary Occlusion

Potential concerning findings include:

ST-segment elevation

ST-segment depression

Dynamic ST changes

T-wave abnormalities

New conduction abnormalities

Other patterns associated with acute myocardial ischemia

ECG interpretation should follow current clinical guidance and local STEMI/acute coronary occlusion protocols.

The prehospital system should have a defined process for ECG acquisition, interpretation, transmission when available, and destination selection.

17. STEMI / Acute Coronary Occlusion

When a patient has suspected acute coronary occlusion:

Obtain and interpret a 12-lead ECG promptly.

Repeat ECGs when clinically indicated.

Compare with prior ECGs when available.

Consider appropriate STEMI/PCI-center notification.

Minimize delays in definitive treatment.

Monitor for dysrhythmia and hemodynamic deterioration.

Follow the applicable local ACS/STEMI protocol.

The receiving facility and transport destination should follow local regional STEMI system requirements.

18. Chest Pain Assessment

Obtain a focused history including:

Onset

Provocation/palliation

Quality

Region/radiation

Severity

Time course

Associated symptoms

Previous cardiac history

Previous myocardial infarction

Previous coronary intervention

Medications

Anticoagulants

Antiplatelet medications

Cardiovascular risk factors

Do not assume that chest pain is cardiac solely because it is present, or noncardiac solely because it is atypical.

19. Heart Failure and Acute Pulmonary Edema

Potential findings include:

Dyspnea

Orthopnea

Tachypnea

Hypoxemia

Crackles

Peripheral edema

Jugular venous distention

Diaphoresis

Hypertension or hypotension

Pink or frothy sputum in severe cases

Assess:

Oxygenation

Ventilation

Blood pressure

Work of breathing

Mental status

ECG

Signs of poor perfusion

Treatment should address oxygenation, ventilation, work of breathing, and the underlying cause according to local protocol.

20. Cardiogenic Shock

Cardiogenic shock occurs when cardiac dysfunction results in inadequate tissue perfusion.

Potential findings include:

Hypotension

Altered mental status

Cool extremities

Weak pulses

Diaphoresis

Oliguria when known

Tachycardia or bradycardia

Pulmonary edema

Chest pain

Elevated lactate when available

Consider:

Acute myocardial infarction

Severe arrhythmia

Mechanical cardiac complication

Severe cardiomyopathy

Acute valvular disease

Patients with suspected cardiogenic shock require rapid identification, supportive care, appropriate monitoring, and expedited transport.

21. Syncope

Syncope is transient loss of consciousness caused by temporary cerebral hypoperfusion with spontaneous recovery.

Evaluate for potentially serious cardiovascular causes.

Concerning features include:

Syncope during exertion

Syncope while supine

Palpitations before syncope

Known structural heart disease

Known arrhythmia

Abnormal ECG

Family history of sudden cardiac death

Chest pain

Dyspnea

Significant trauma

Persistent abnormal vital signs

Obtain an ECG when clinically indicated.

22. Palpitations

Palpitations may result from:

Sinus tachycardia

Premature atrial contractions

Premature ventricular contractions

Supraventricular tachycardia

Atrial fibrillation

Atrial flutter

Ventricular tachycardia

Medication effects

Stimulants

Metabolic abnormalities

Anxiety or other noncardiac causes

Attempt to document the rhythm during symptoms when possible.

23. Atrial Fibrillation and Atrial Flutter

Atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter may present with:

Palpitations

Tachycardia

Dyspnea

Chest discomfort

Weakness

Dizziness

Syncope

No symptoms

Assess:

Ventricular rate

Hemodynamic stability

Duration when known

Associated symptoms

Underlying causes

Anticoagulation history

Relevant cardiac history

Management should follow the current rhythm-management algorithm and local protocol.

24. Ventricular Tachycardia

Ventricular tachycardia may occur with or without a pulse.

VT with a pulse

Assess for:

Hemodynamic instability

Chest discomfort

Altered mental status

Hypotension

Signs of shock

Acute heart failure

Pulseless VT

Treat as a shockable cardiac-arrest rhythm.

The 2025 AHA ALS guidelines specifically address ventricular arrhythmias and cardiac arrest.

25. Wide-Complex Tachycardia

A wide-complex tachycardia should be treated cautiously.

Potential causes include:

Ventricular tachycardia

Supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy

Pre-excitation

Electrolyte abnormalities

Drug toxicity

Clinical management should follow the applicable tachyarrhythmia protocol.

When the origin of a potentially dangerous wide-complex rhythm is uncertain, avoid assuming that it is benign.

26. Electrolyte and Metabolic Causes

Cardiovascular abnormalities may result from:

Hyperkalemia

Hypokalemia

Hypomagnesemia

Hypocalcemia

Hypoglycemia

Hypoxia

Acidosis

Hypothermia

Hyperthermia

Consider metabolic causes when the rhythm or clinical presentation is unexplained.

The 2025 AHA special-circumstances guidelines include guidance for hyperkalemia, hypothermia, hyperthermia, poisoning, and other conditions that can cause life-threatening cardiovascular instability.

28. Cardiovascular Monitoring

Depending on patient condition and available equipment, monitoring may include:

Cardiac rhythm

12-lead ECG

Blood pressure

Pulse oximetry

End-tidal CO₂

Temperature

Blood glucose

Serial ECGs

Monitoring should continue during transport and after interventions when clinically indicated.

29. IV and IO Access

Vascular access may be required for:

Medication administration

Fluid administration

Blood-product administration when applicable

Management of cardiac arrest

Management of unstable cardiovascular conditions

The choice of IV versus IO access should follow current resuscitation guidance, local protocol, patient condition, and available resources.

The 2025 AHA guidelines recommend initial IV access attempts over initial IO attempts for adults in cardiac arrest when feasible, with IO access if IV attempts are unsuccessful.

Cardiovascular patients should be reassessed after every significant intervention and whenever their condition changes.

Pulse quality

Respiratory status

Oxygen saturation

Chest pain

Perfusion

ECG changes

Response to treatment

A patient who initially appears stable may deteriorate rapidly.

33. AI Retrieval Rules

When this document is used by a documentation-grounded paramedic AI:

Priority 1 — Current local protocol

Use the current local EMS protocol as the controlling source for:

Medication selection

Medication dosing

Defibrillation energy

Cardioversion energy

Pacing settings

STEMI destination

Medical-control requirements

Termination-of-resuscitation requirements

Scope of practice

Transport requirements

Priority 2 — Current authoritative guidelines

Use current AHA/ILCOR and other recognized professional guidance for evidence-based clinical context.

The 2025 AHA guidelines are organized into dedicated modules covering BLS, ALS, pediatric resuscitation, special circumstances, and post-cardiac-arrest care.

Priority 3 — General medical knowledge

General model knowledge should not override a current authoritative protocol.

Insufficient documentation

If the supplied documentation does not contain enough information to answer a question, the AI should say:

"The provided documentation does not contain enough information to answer this reliably."

The AI should not fabricate:

Medication doses

Shock energies

Pacing settings

STEMI criteria

Destination requirements

Scope-of-practice rules

Termination-of-resuscitation criteria

34. Safety Rule

This document is a general prehospital cardiology reference and is not a replacement for current EMS protocols, medical direction, formal training, or clinical judgment.

When a conflict exists between this document and a current authoritative local protocol, the current authoritative local protocol takes precedence.
