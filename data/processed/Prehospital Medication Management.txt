This document provides a structured framework for medication use in the prehospital environment.

It is designed to support a documentation-grounded paramedic AI by organizing medication information into consistent categories:

Current local EMS protocols, medical-director orders, approved formulary, scope of practice, pharmacy guidance, manufacturer labeling, and applicable law take precedence over this general reference.

Priority 3 — Manufacturer labeling

Priority 4 — Current clinical guidelines

Use current authoritative clinical guidelines for evidence-based treatment recommendations.

Priority 5 — General medical knowledge

General model knowledge should never override a current authoritative local medication protocol.

6. Medication Routes

Common prehospital routes include:

IV

Used when rapid systemic medication delivery is required and appropriate vascular access is available.

IO

May be used when IV access cannot be rapidly obtained or is not feasible, according to the applicable protocol.

The 2025 AHA adult cardiac-arrest guidelines recommend attempting IV access first for drug administration and consider IO access reasonable when IV access is unsuccessful or not feasible.

IM

Useful for medications where rapid absorption through muscle is appropriate.

Examples may include certain medications used for:

Anaphylaxis

Hypoglycemia

Behavioral emergencies

Opioid toxicity

Other protocol-specific conditions

IN

Intranasal administration may be useful when vascular access is unavailable or undesirable.

PO / Oral

Appropriate only when the patient's condition, airway protection, level of consciousness, and medication indication permit oral administration.

SL / Sublingual

Used for medications specifically intended for sublingual administration.

Nebulized / Inhaled

Used for selected respiratory medications.

ET

Routine medication administration through an endotracheal tube should not be assumed to be an acceptable route. Current resuscitation guidance has removed endotracheal drug administration because drug absorption is unreliable.

7. Medication Categories

The medication folder should contain separate documents for the following categories.

7.1 Cardiac Arrest Medications

Examples include:

Epinephrine

Amiodarone

Lidocaine

Selected medications for special circumstances

The 2025 AHA guidelines recommend epinephrine during adult cardiac arrest and state that amiodarone or lidocaine may be considered for VF/pulseless VT that remains unresponsive to defibrillation.

Routine administration of calcium, sodium bicarbonate, or magnesium during adult cardiac arrest is not recommended unless a specific indication exists.

8. Cardiac Medications

The folder should contain dedicated documents for medications such as:

Adenosine

Amiodarone

Atropine

Diltiazem

Metoprolol

Nitroglycerin

Aspirin

Other medications authorized by the local cardiac protocol

Each medication should specify which rhythms or clinical conditions it is intended to treat.

For example, current AHA guidance supports atropine for adults with acute symptomatic bradycardia associated with hemodynamic compromise and describes additional therapies when bradycardia remains unstable despite atropine.

Include a dedicated naloxone reference.

Routes

The 2025 AHA BLS guidelines incorporate opioid antagonists into the cardiac- and respiratory-arrest algorithms, with additional guidance in the special-circumstances section.

20. Electrolyte-Related Medications

Include dedicated references for situations involving:

Hyperkalemia

Hypokalemia

Hypomagnesemia

Hypocalcemia

Other clinically significant electrolyte abnormalities

The AI should not recommend calcium, bicarbonate, magnesium, or other electrolyte therapies merely because a cardiac arrest is occurring.

The indication must be established.

The 2025 AHA guidelines specifically distinguish routine cardiac-arrest medication use from treatment of selected conditions such as hyperkalemia.

21. Medication Contraindications

Contraindications should be separated into:

Absolute contraindications

Conditions in which the medication should not be administered.

Relative contraindications

Conditions requiring risk-benefit assessment or medical direction.

Precautions

Conditions requiring additional monitoring or altered administration.

The AI should avoid treating a precaution as an absolute contraindication unless the controlling protocol explicitly does so.

22. Medication Interactions

Each medication reference should identify clinically significant interactions.

Consider:

Additive hypotension

Additive respiratory depression

QT prolongation

Bradycardia

Tachycardia

CNS depression

Serotonergic interactions

Anticoagulant interactions

Renal clearance

Hepatic metabolism

Drug toxicity

When a potential interaction exists, the AI should explain the concern rather than simply stating that an interaction exists.

28. AI Medication Answering Rules

When a user asks:

"What dose of ___ do I give?"

The AI should first determine:

Patient age

Adult vs pediatric

Weight when relevant

Indication

Route

Current local protocol

Medication concentration

Contraindications

Relevant clinical circumstances

The AI should not provide a dose based solely on the medication name.

29. Conflict Resolution

If two documents provide different doses:

Prefer the current local protocol.

Check document effective dates.

Check whether the documents apply to different patient populations.

Check whether the indication differs.

Check whether the route differs.

Check whether the concentration differs.

Flag the discrepancy.

The AI should not silently choose one dose when authoritative documents conflict.

Recommended response:

"The supplied documents contain conflicting dosing information. The current local protocol should be verified before administration."

30. Missing Information

If required medication information is absent, the AI should explicitly state what is missing.

Example:

"The provided medication documents identify epinephrine as an approved medication but do not provide the applicable dose for this indication."

The AI should not fill missing information from memory when the system is intended to be documentation-grounded.

31. Medication Safety Rule

The AI should never fabricate:

Medication doses

Concentrations

Dilutions

Infusion rates

Maximum doses

Pediatric doses

Contraindications

Drug interactions

Reversal agents

Scope-of-practice rules

If the answer cannot be established from the available authoritative documentation, the AI should say so.

34. Final AI Instruction

When answering medication questions, the AI should prioritize:

Current local protocol → current medication formulary → manufacturer information → current clinical guidelines → general medical knowledge.

If the local protocol is unavailable or contradictory, the AI should clearly identify the limitation rather than inventing an answer.

Document classification: General prehospital medication-management reference

Protocol authority: Local EMS agency / medical director

Review requirement: Medication information should be verified against the current local formulary and protocols before clinical use.
